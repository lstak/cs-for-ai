Module 9

Peripherals & I/O

A CPU on its own can only compute. Keyboards, cameras, microphones, speakers, displays and network cards are how a computer perceives and acts, and so they are the senses and hands of every AI system: a voice assistant listens through a microphone, a photo app sees through a camera, a robot acts through motors.

  • Explain buses, controllers, drivers and how a device talks to the CPU.
  • Compare polling and interrupts, and explain DMA.
  • Describe how a voice assistant or camera-based AI turns real-world signals into a model's input and back into action.
  • Check whether an interface can carry the data rate of a sensor (e.g. a camera) before you buy or design.

1. The motherboard as a city map

Components are joined by buses, shared data highways with different speeds and purposes:

  • Memory bus: CPU ↔ RAM. Very fast.
  • PCIe: the high-speed expansion bus. GPUs, NVMe SSDs and fast network cards plug in here. Measured in "lanes" (×4, ×16); more lanes, more bandwidth.
  • USB: the universal connector for keyboards, webcams, drives, microphones, microcontrollers. Versions differ hugely in speed.
  • SATA / NVMe: storage links. NVMe (over PCIe) is much faster than SATA.
  • Ethernet / Wi-Fi: network (Module 6).
  • I²C, SPI, UART, GPIO, MIPI-CSI: simple links used by sensors and cameras on embedded boards (Raspberry Pi, Jetson, microcontrollers).

Each device has a controller (a small chip speaking its protocol) and the OS has a driver (software that knows how to talk to that controller and presents a uniform interface, e.g. "a camera", "a disk").

2. How does the CPU know something happened?

You press a key. How does the program find out? Two strategies:

  • Polling: the CPU keeps asking "anything yet?" at intervals. Simple, but wastes effort when nothing is happening, and reacts late if the interval is long.
  • Interrupts: the device sends an electrical signal. The CPU pauses what it is doing, runs a short handler, and goes back. Reaction is immediate and idle time costs nothing.

DMA: don't make the CPU carry the boxes

Copying a 4 MB camera frame into memory one byte at a time would keep the CPU busy full-time. Direct Memory Access (DMA) lets a device (or controller) write straight into RAM by itself and interrupt the CPU only once at the end. The same idea lets GPUs and network cards move large buffers without the CPU. Your GPU uses DMA to receive the model's weights and your inputs from RAM, and a network card uses it to deliver an incoming reply into memory.

3. Can the wire keep up?

Every interface has a maximum throughput. A sensor produces a data stream at a rate you can compute: pixels × bytes per pixel × frames per second × number of cameras. If the stream is more than the interface can carry, frames are dropped or compressed (and compression costs CPU/GPU time and can hurt your model's accuracy).

Edge AI means running the model right next to the sensor (a phone, a doorbell camera, a car, a drone) instead of sending everything to the cloud. It is an I/O decision as much as a compute one: raw video may be too much to upload (see the widget), the reply may need to arrive in tens of milliseconds, and some data (a baby monitor, a medical device) shouldn't leave the building at all. The usual compromise is to run a small model locally and call a bigger one in the cloud only when needed.

4. Case study: a voice assistant

Saying "what's the weather tomorrow?" to a smart speaker chains almost every idea in this course:

  1. Microphone samples the air pressure 16,000 times a second (Module 1): 16,000 samples × 2 bytes = 32 KB/s, tiny compared with a camera. A small always-on model listens for the wake word; nothing is sent anywhere before it hears it.
  2. Speech-to-text model turns the audio numbers into text (on the device or in the cloud, Module 12).
  3. A language model (Module 11) decides what to do. It may request a tool, such as a weather lookup (Module 13) over the network (Module 6).
  4. Text-to-speech model turns the reply text back into audio samples.
  5. The speaker (a peripheral again) plays them, ideally starting before the whole reply is generated (streaming).

Each stage adds a delay, and the stages run one after the other, so a pause of 1–2 seconds is common. Designers shorten it by streaming between stages, running small models on the device, and keeping a persistent connection to the cloud (Module 6). It is the same pipeline thinking (what happens, in which order, at what cost) that you'll apply in the capstone.
